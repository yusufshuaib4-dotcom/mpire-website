Notes from the field

Practical positions, not thought leadership for its own sake.

R&D 6 min read

A proof of concept should be allowed to fail

The purpose of a proof of concept is to answer one question honestly: is the riskiest assumption behind this idea true. Too many organisations design their pilots to succeed instead, quietly narrowing scope and cherry-picking test conditions until a positive result is guaranteed. That protects a budget line for another quarter. It does not protect the business from building on a foundation that will not hold.

Our practice is to name the assumption we are actually testing before any code is written, agree with the client on what a negative result would mean, and hold the test to that standard even when the early signs look encouraging. A proof of concept that cannot fail was never a test in the first place. It was a demo with better paperwork.

Consultancy 5 min read

Design the pilot team before you design the pilot

Most AI pilots are scoped as a technical exercise: pick a use case, pick a model, measure an accuracy number. The organisational question, who owns the pilot once it leaves the lab and who has the authority to change a business process around it, is usually answered later, if at all. By then the pilot has either quietly stalled for lack of an owner, or succeeded and immediately exposed that no one was resourced to operationalise it.

We ask clients to name the pilot's eventual operational owner before the first line of code is written, not after the results come in. It is an uncomfortable conversation to have early. It is a far more expensive one to have after the technology has already proven itself and stalled anyway.

Programming 7 min read

The real cost of hosting your own model

Self-hosting a large language model is often framed as a data-sovereignty decision, and sometimes it is exactly that. But the total cost of the decision rarely appears on the same page as the sovereignty argument. Hosting introduces a standing commitment to GPU capacity, model versioning, and a security posture that a managed API absorbs on your behalf. The comparison that matters is not licence cost versus infrastructure cost. It is who on your team will own that infrastructure at 2 a.m. when it needs attention.

We help clients make this trade-off explicitly, sizing the operational commitment against the actual sensitivity of the data involved, rather than defaulting to self-hosting because it sounds more serious or to a managed API because it is faster to start with.

Consultancy 5 min read

Technical debt is a planning input, not a confession

Engineering teams tend to disclose technical debt apologetically, as though it were evidence of past failure. Framed that way, it gets minimised in front of decision-makers, and the roadmap gets built on an inaccurate picture of what the system can actually support. Debt is better treated as a planning input with a cost and an interest rate, the same as any other constraint a delivery plan has to account for.

When we review a codebase or an architecture, we quantify debt in terms of what it will cost to work around versus what it will cost to resolve, and let the client make an informed trade-off. Neither answer is inherently correct. An uninformed one is the only real risk.
